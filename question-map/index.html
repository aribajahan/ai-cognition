AI and cognition: A Question Map

What researchers are investigating, how cognition is being measured, and what remains unanswered.

By Ariba Jahan · Last updated September 24, 2026 · Unmissables · LinkedIn

While writing Cognitive Endurance, I kept seeing studies and headlines about AI weakening memory, learning, and critical thinking. I wanted to understand what researchers were investigating, how those abilities were being measured, and which questions were still open. The field is moving quickly, so I also wanted a baseline I could return to as the evidence changes.

This map brings together 110 source records found through Claude Science and Perplexity. I checked each source against the question it appears under and kept the study design, sample, timing, and limits visible. The search was exploratory and did not follow a systematic-review protocol.

Chan et al. 2024 arXiv (Cornell University)
AI studyPrimary studypreprint

Watching a crime video followed by generative chatbot interaction caused over three times more immediate false memories than control, with 36.4% of responses misled; these persisted one week later.

Researchers had people go through a simulated witness interview using a generative chatbot, a pre-scripted chatbot, a survey, or no interview (control), then checked for false memories, memories of things that didn't happen. The generative chatbot produced over 3 times more immediate false memories than the control and 1.7 times more than the survey, and it misled 36.4% of users' responses during the conversation. A week later, the number of false memories from the generative chatbot stayed the same, and people's confidence in these false memories stayed higher than in the control group, suggesting the effect didn't fade over time.

Kosmyna et al. 2025 arXiv (Cornell University)
AI studyPrimary studypreprint

Among 54 adults writing essays across three sessions, the LLM group showed the lowest task-time EEG connectivity; immediately afterward, 15 of 18 could not accurately quote their essay, versus 2 of 18 in each comparison group. The preprint does not establish lasting brain or learning change.

Kosmyna and colleagues measured brain connectivity, or how strongly different brain regions' electrical signals sync up, while people wrote essays either alone, with a search engine, or with an LLM (large language model, like ChatGPT). The more outside help people used, the weaker and narrower their brain connectivity became: unaided writers showed the strongest, widest networks, search-engine users were in between, and LLM users showed the weakest coupling overall. In a fourth session, people who had used an LLM first and then switched to writing alone still showed weaker connectivity and under-used alpha and beta brain-wave networks, while people who switched from writing alone to using an LLM recalled more and re-engaged widespread brain regions at the back and front of the head. Behaviorally, 83.3% of the LLM group (15 of 18) couldn't correctly quote from the essay they had just written, compared with only 11.1% (2 of 18) in each of the other two groups.

Gilbert et al. 2022 Psychonomic Bulletin & Review
Cognitive psychologyReviewpeer-reviewed

People systematically set more reminders than optimal, driven by metacognitive beliefs rather than actual ability. Reminders work well, but older adults don't fully compensate for memory decline.

People forgot about 45% of the time when they relied on their own memory, but only about 5% of the time when they used an external reminder. Whether someone chose to set a reminder was tied to their beliefs about how good their memory is, not to how good their memory actually was (r = -0.01) — a mismatch the authors link to a broader tendency to lean on reminders based on gut feelings about memory, wanting to avoid effort, and sticking with familiar habits, rather than to any causal harm or benefit from using reminders. This pattern shifts with age: children under about 9 know they might forget but don't yet act on that by setting reminders, while older adults tend to be overconfident and under-use reminders and younger adults tend to be underconfident and over-rely on them. The authors note these age-related patterns come from studies comparing different people at one point in time, not from tracking the same people over years, so they're less certain.

Fan et al. 2024 British Journal of Educational Technology
AI studyPrimary studypeer-reviewed

ChatGPT improved essay scores versus expert chat, writing tools, or none, but didn't boost knowledge gain, transfer, or motivation—possibly fostering reliance over deeper learning.

Fan and colleagues randomly assigned students to get help from ChatGPT, a human expert chat, writing analytics tools, or no extra tool while working on a writing task. Researchers had people do an X-ray luggage-screening task while getting advice from either a human or a machine helper, and giving bad advice made people both worse at the task and less likely to use the advice, F(1,22) = 7.63, p = 0.011 for advice use and F(2,31) = 13.85, p < 0.0001 for task performance. Surprisingly, people cut back on trusting the human advisor more sharply over time than the machine advisor, F(1,22) = 5.06, p = 0.035. This happened because people initially rated the human as far more reliable than its actual 60% reliability, t(11) = 6.79, p < 0.0001, then sharply lowered that rating after seeing it give wrong advice, t(11) = 4.10, p = 0.002. The authors suggest people over-trust human advisors compared to automated ones at first, but recalibrate, or adjust, that trust more sharply once the human's mistakes show up — a pattern also linked to stronger brain-connectivity signals from certain "driver" hub regions during bad advice when the advisor was human.
